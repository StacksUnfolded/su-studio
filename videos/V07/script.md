## NARRATION

### 0. Cold open

*[FRAME 1 = THE THUMBNAIL MOOD. Night. You, sitting on the edge of a bed, phone face-down on your knee. Rain on the window. The counter slams in: DAY 1,826.]*

Day one thousand, eight hundred and twenty-six.

Exactly five years ago tonight, you won ten million dollars.

*[Close on the phone. Your thumb hovers over a banking app with no readable name.]*

And right now, your banking app is open… and you're scared to look at it.

*[Split screen. Right side: Dez, same night, two states away, sitting on the hood of a car with a FOR SALE sign on it.]*

Two states away, the only other person who won that night is checking his.

*[Beat. Clock tick.]*

You both started with the same ticket. The same jackpot. The same five years.

One of you is still rich. One of you isn't.

*[Seven blank lottery-ticket cards fan out across the screen.]*

The difference comes down to seven rules. Not luck. Not a genius investment. Seven boring rules, and one study about lottery winners that should scare anyone who ever gets a windfall.

So let's go back to day zero. And this time, you get to watch every decision.

### Title card

*[Title card: POV: YOU WIN $10 MILLION. Calendar rips back to DAY 0.]*

### 1. Day 0: The ticket

*[Chapter card: DAY 0 · THE TICKET.]*

*[A small kitchen at 11pm. You in pyjamas, eating cereal, a crumpled ticket on the table. A TV on the counter with numbered balls on screen, no logos.]*

It's a Wednesday. You bought the ticket on the way home because the jackpot hit twenty million dollars and everyone at work was talking about it.

*[The balls drop one at a time. You check them against the ticket. Spoon freezes halfway to your mouth.]*

First number. Match. Second. Match.

[short pause] Third, fourth, fifth.

[short pause] The last ball.

*[Cereal everywhere.]*

Match.

*[The TV shows "2 WINNING TICKETS".]*

Two tickets matched that night. The twenty million is split down the middle. Your half: ten million dollars.

*[You grab your phone to call everyone you know. Your thumb hovers over "Mom".]*

And here is the first moment that decides how this ends. You want to call your mom. Your best friend. Your group chat. Your boss, so you can quit.

*[You put the phone down. Slowly. You slide the ticket into a book on the shelf.]*

You don't call anyone.

Because the second people know, your life stops being yours. You haven't got a plan yet, so for now the only plan is silence.

*[Split screen. Dez, same night, filming himself screaming with the ticket. A post with a rocket emoji. Notifications explode.]*

Dez posts a video of his ticket. With the numbers showing.

Rule number one: say nothing until you have a plan.

*[Stamp: RULE 1 · SAY NOTHING UNTIL YOU HAVE A PLAN.]*

### 2. Day 1: The real number

*[Chapter card: DAY 1 · THE REAL NUMBER.]*

*[You at the kitchen table at dawn, wide awake, a calculator and a legal pad.]*

The next morning you do something most winners skip. You work out how much you actually won.

*[A giant gold "$10,000,000" fills the screen.]*

That ten million on the billboard? It isn't a pile of cash. It's the annuity number. One payment now, then twenty-nine more payments, one a year, each five percent bigger than the last.

*[Source pill: "Annuity: 1 payment + 29 yearly, rising 5% a year · Powerball.com".]*

Or you can take the cash option: one payment, today. And that's usually a lot less than half. When one jackpot was advertised at five hundred and sixty-five million dollars, the cash option was about two hundred and fifty-five million. Roughly forty-five percent.

*[Source pill: "$565M jackpot, cash value ~$255.3M (Aug 2025) · Powerball.com".]*

*[The money waterfall starts. HYPOTHETICAL stamp.]*

So you take the cash. Ten million becomes four and a half.

Then, before you ever see a cent, the lottery holds back twenty-four percent for federal tax. That's the rule for big gambling wins.

*[Source pill: "24% federal withholding on lottery wins over $5,000 · IRS, Form W-2G instructions".]*

That's another million gone. Three point four million lands in your account.

*[Your eyes light up. Then a small sticky note appears on the screen: "APRIL".]*

But that twenty-four percent is just a down payment. A win this big puts you in the top tax bracket, thirty-seven percent, so next April you'll owe roughly another half a million dollars.

*[Source pill: "37% top federal rate, single filers above $640,600 (2026) · IRS".]*

And that's in a state with no income tax. In many states, there's a state bill on top.

*[The waterfall ends on one green number: "$2.9M".]*

Your ten million dollars is about two point nine million.

Still life-changing. But it's not ten. And every bad decision winners make starts with spending like the big number is real.

*[You open a second bank account and move six hundred thousand dollars into it. You label it with a drawing of a tax form.]*

So the first thing you buy with your winnings is… nothing. You move the April tax money into its own account, where you can't see it every day.

*[Split screen. Dez at a car dealership, pointing at three cars.]*

Dez is still spending from the ten million in his head.

Rule number two: your real number is the one after tax.

*[Stamp: RULE 2 · YOUR REAL NUMBER IS AFTER TAX.]*

### 3. Week 1: The team

*[Chapter card: WEEK 1 · THE TEAM.]*

*[An office with three chairs across a desk. You sit opposite three new cast members: a planner with a folder, a tax accountant with a calculator, a lawyer with a briefcase.]*

You still haven't claimed the prize. Most lotteries give you months, not days, so there's time to do it properly.

First, you hire three people: a fee-only financial planner, who's paid by you and not by commissions on what they sell you; a tax accountant; and a lawyer.

*[A checklist ticks itself: planner, accountant, lawyer.]*

You interview several of each. You ask how they get paid. And you notice which ones get excited about your money, and which ones get excited about a plan.

*[A map of the US, about a dozen states glowing.]*

Then the lawyer tells you something you didn't know. In about a dozen states, jackpot winners can stay completely anonymous. In others, your name and your town can become public record, and some states only offer privacy above a certain prize size.

*[Source pill: "About 12 states allow full anonymity; more above a prize threshold · jackpot.com, July 2026".]*

*[You at the lottery office in a plain grey hoodie, signing a form. No cameras.]*

You live in a state where you can claim quietly, so you do.

*[Split screen. Dez on a stage holding a giant cardboard check, camera flashes going off. His face is on the evening news.]*

Dez does a press conference with a giant check.

By Friday, he has two thousand new friend requests.

Rule number three: build the team before you spend a dollar.

*[Stamp: RULE 3 · BUILD THE TEAM BEFORE YOU SPEND.]*

### 4. Month 1: Everyone you've ever met

*[Chapter card: MONTH 1 · EVERYONE YOU'VE EVER MET.]*

*[A family dinner. Everyone smiling a bit too hard. A cousin slides a business plan across the mashed potatoes.]*

You did stay quiet. But people find out. They always do.

And suddenly, everyone you've ever met needs something. A cousin has a business idea. An old friend's car broke down. A guy from high school wants to "catch up over coffee". Your phone lights up with numbers you don't know.

*[Phone screen: invented messages pile up. "Congrats!!!", "Long time no see", "Investment opportunity 🚀", "You've been selected…".]*

And here's the trap: every single request is small compared to millions. Ten thousand here, fifty thousand there. Saying yes feels easy.

Fifty yeses later, a million dollars is gone.

*[You and the planner at a table. A pie chart labelled only "GIVING" glows.]*

So you make one decision, once, in writing. Ten percent of your real number goes to family and friends. You decide exactly who gets what: your parents' mortgage, a college fund for your nephew, something for your best friend.

*[HYPOTHETICAL stamp. On screen: "10% of $2.9M ≈ $290K".]*

And you learn a tax rule. In 2026, you can give anyone up to nineteen thousand dollars a year without even filing a form. Bigger gifts need a gift tax return, but you won't actually owe gift tax unless your lifetime gifts go past fifteen million dollars.

*[Source pill: "$19,000 annual gift exclusion; $15M lifetime exemption (2026) · IRS".]*

When the list is done, it's done. Anyone new gets the same answer: "All of that goes through my planner now."

*[Split screen. Dez handing out cash at a family barbecue. A long line of people behind him.]*

Dez says yes to everyone. The line at his door is getting longer.

Rule number four: decide who gets what once, in writing.

*[Stamp: RULE 4 · DECIDE WHO GETS WHAT ONCE.]*

### 5. Month 3: The house

*[Chapter card: MONTH 3 · THE HOUSE.]*

*[A real estate agent with a huge smile shows you a mansion with columns and a fountain.]*

Of course, you want a house. Everyone does.

The agent shows you a mansion. Four million dollars. You could almost afford it… if you didn't need the money for anything else.

*[A transparent bill floats over the mansion, then splits into many small bills: property tax, insurance, pool cleaner, heating, a gardener.]*

But a house isn't one price. It's a monthly bill that never stops. Property tax, insurance, repairs, a roof that needs replacing, a pool that needs cleaning. The bigger the house, the bigger the bill, every month, forever.

*[A nice, normal family house with a small garden. You standing in front of it with keys.]*

So you buy a nice, normal house for six hundred thousand dollars, in cash. No mortgage.

*[HYPOTHETICAL stamp. On screen: "House $600K · upkeep ≈ $1,250 a month".]*

The upkeep is about twelve hundred and fifty dollars a month. You can carry that for the rest of your life without thinking about it.

*[Split screen. Dez in front of a huge mansion with a jet ski, a boat and three sports cars in the drive.]*

Dez buys the mansion. And a boat. And a second boat for the first boat.

Rule number five: every purchase comes with a monthly bill.

*[Stamp: RULE 5 · EVERY PURCHASE COMES WITH A MONTHLY BILL.]*

### 6. Month 6: The job

*[Chapter card: MONTH 6 · THE JOB.]*

*[Your old office. You at your desk, a coffee, your boss walking past. You're smiling to yourself.]*

You still go to work. And everyone thinks you're weird for it.

Here's why. After the gifts and the house, just under two million dollars is left to invest. That sounds like you never have to work again.

*[A tree grows out of a pile of coins. Fruit drops into a basket. Callback to V03.]*

But remember the old money rule: eat the fruit, never the tree. A pile like this might pay out somewhere around three to four percent a year without shrinking, if things go well.

*[HYPOTHETICAL stamp. On screen: "$1.85M × 4% ≈ $74,000 a year".]*

That's about seventy thousand dollars a year.

*[You in a calm home office, a laptop showing a simple calendar.]*

That's a great life. It's just not a "never think about money again" life. So you switch to part-time, take a pay cut, and let the pile do some of the work.

*[Split screen. Dez burning a piece of paper labelled with a briefcase icon: his resignation. Confetti.]*

Dez quit his job on day two. In a video. He called his boss a clown.

Rule number six: your new salary is what the pile pays you, not the pile.

*[Stamp: RULE 6 · YOUR SALARY IS WHAT THE PILE PAYS YOU.]*

### Quick catch-up

*[Six tickets fan out quickly, one per beat.]*

Quick catch-up. You told no one. You worked out the real number and hid the tax money. You built a team, made one giving list, bought a house you can afford forever, and kept your job.

Dez has done the exact opposite of all six. And so far, honestly? He's having a lot more fun than you are.

*[Beat.]*

But it's about to be April.

### 7. Year 1: April

*[Chapter card: YEAR 1 · APRIL.]*

*[Your accountant's office. A tax bill slides across the desk. You move money across from the labelled account. Done in one click.]*

Tax season. Remember that half a million dollars? Your accountant files, you pay from the account you set up on day one, and you go home for dinner.

Total stress: about ten minutes.

*[Split screen. Dez opening an envelope at his kitchen island. His face drains.]*

Dez gets the same bill.

*[Dez's BANK number in red, dropping fast.]*

But his money is in the boat. And the cars. And in loans to forty-one people he'll never see again.

He sells one of the cars at a loss to pay it. Then the second boat. He's still short.

### 8. Year 2: The opportunity

*[Chapter card: YEAR 2 · THE OPPORTUNITY.]*

*[A trendy pop-up restaurant. Your friend Marco, in an apron, shows you a sketch of a menu.]*

Year two. Your friend Marco wants to open a restaurant. He's good. He's passionate. He needs two hundred thousand dollars.

And you really want to say yes.

*[A small jar appears, labelled with a party popper icon. HYPOTHETICAL stamp: "Fun money: 5% ≈ $140K".]*

But back in week one, your planner made you set up a fun-money bucket: five percent of the winnings. That's the money for risky ideas, toys and friends' dreams. When it's empty, it's empty.

So you offer Marco fifty thousand from the bucket. Not two hundred.

*[Time-lapse: the restaurant opens, gets busy, then slowly empties. A CLOSED sign.]*

Eighteen months later, the restaurant closes. You're sad for Marco. But you're not ruined.

*[Split screen. Dez shaking hands with a man in a shiny suit in front of a slide titled with a rocket and a chart. Dez hands over a huge stack of cash.]*

Dez puts a million dollars into a "can't-lose" crypto fund run by a guy he met at a car show.

You can guess how that goes.

Rule number seven: only bet money you've already said goodbye to.

*[Stamp: RULE 7 · ONLY BET MONEY YOU'VE SAID GOODBYE TO.]*

### 9. Year 3: The danger zone

*[Chapter card: YEAR 3 · THE DANGER ZONE.]*

*[A grey university-style room. A whiteboard with a simple line chart.]*

Now, you've probably heard that seventy percent of lottery winners go broke. It's everywhere online.

*[The "70%" appears in big numbers, then cracks.]*

But the organization that statistic is usually pinned on says it isn't their research, and they can't confirm it.

*[Source pill: "NEFE (Jan 2018): the 70% figure 'is not backed by research from NEFE'".]*

There's a real study, though. And it's scarier.

*[A sun-shaped map of Florida with lots of tiny ticket icons.]*

Researchers followed about thirty-five thousand lottery winners in Florida. They compared people who won fifty to a hundred and fifty thousand dollars with people who won less than ten thousand.

Right after winning, the bigger winners were less likely to go bankrupt. Makes sense: they had cash.

*[A line chart. The big-winner line starts low, then climbs to meet the small-winner line around years three to five.]*

But three to five years later, the advantage was gone. The money had mostly delayed bankruptcy instead of preventing it. The bigger winners didn't use the win to pay off their debts or build up savings.

*[Source pill: "Hankins, Hoekstra & Skiba, Review of Economics and Statistics, 2011".]*

To be fair, those were much smaller wins than yours. But the pattern is the point: a windfall doesn't fix money habits. It just gives them more fuel.

*[You at home, in front of a laptop showing a fancy watch. Your cursor hovers over BUY.]*

And year three is when you feel it too. The excitement's gone. You're a bit bored. You start to wonder why you're living like a normal person when you have millions.

*[You close the laptop.]*

You don't buy the watch. You don't buy the bigger house. That's the hardest decision of the whole five years, and nobody will ever congratulate you for it.

### 10. Year 4: Dez

*[Chapter card: YEAR 4 · DEZ.]*

*[Dez's mansion with a FOR SALE sign. The boats are gone. One car left.]*

Let's check in on Dez.

He's sold the mansion. He's selling the last car. Most of the people who lined up at his door don't answer his calls anymore. And the crypto guy's website doesn't exist.

*[HYPOTHETICAL stamp. Dez's BANK number ticks down to "$0".]*

He didn't do one big crazy thing. He did dozens of medium ones, in the first year, all spending from the ten million that was never really there.

*[Dez sitting alone on the curb, looking at an empty wallet.]*

He's not a villain. He's what happens when anyone gets a lot of money with no rules around it.

### 11. Day 1,826: The app

*[Chapter card: DAY 1,826 · THE APP. Back in the bedroom from the cold open. Rain on the window.]*

So here we are. Five years to the day.

You flip your phone over and open the app.

*[The phone screen: invented banking app. The investment line counts up. HYPOTHETICAL stamp.]*

Two point one million dollars invested. A house with no mortgage. And a job you actually like, three days a week.

*[On screen: "$2.1M invested + house paid off" (HYPOTHETICAL).]*

You still have more invested than you did in month six, even after five years of taking money out.

*[Wide shot: the rain stops. You put the phone down and smile.]*

Five years ago you won ten million dollars. And honestly? The ten million was never the prize. The prize is that you'll never have to worry about money again, as long as you keep the rules.

### 12. The payoff

*[The seven lottery-ticket cards line up across the screen.]*

So here are the seven rules that kept you rich.

One: say nothing until you have a plan.
Two: your real number is the one after tax.
Three: build the team before you spend a dollar.
Four: decide who gets what once, in writing.
Five: every purchase comes with a monthly bill.
Six: your new salary is what the pile pays you.
Seven: only bet money you've already said goodbye to.

*[All but three tickets fade. The remaining three glow gold.]*

And here's the thing. You don't need to win the lottery to use three of these.

Your next raise, your tax refund, a bonus, an inheritance: work out the real number after tax before you spend a cent of it. Every time you buy something, ask what it costs every month, not just today. And if you want to take a risk, decide how much you're willing to lose before you start.

*[Split screen one last time. You at a barbecue in your normal garden. Dez at the same barbecue, laughing, holding a burger. They're friends now.]*

Oh, and Dez? He's fine. He's got a job again. He came to your barbecue last week.

He brought a lottery ticket.

*[Beat. You take it from him and slide it into the book on the shelf.]*

So, be honest. If you won ten million dollars tonight, what's the first thing you'd buy? Tell me in the comments.

*[End screen.]*

And if you want to see how families stay rich for a hundred years instead of five, watch this one next.
