## THE NEW EDIT STYLE (same art, different storytelling)

V04 told the story *around* the host. V05 tells it **through his phone**. BNPL lives inside an app, so the app becomes the main visual.

1. **Phone-first.**
   - A big animated phone, built in code (no AI images), sits beside SU01 for roughly 40% of the video.
   - It shows checkout screens with a fat **PAY IN 4** button, the plans dashboard filling up, the due-date calendar, the bank balance, the credit report and a storm of notifications.
   - It's an invented generic app with no brand name or logo. Its icon is a clock cut into 4 slices.
2. **Debt Tracker HUD** (replaces the level bar): a lock-screen-style widget, top right, showing `PLANS 4 · OWED $512 · NEXT: FRI $63`. It ticks up with a cash-register sound every time a plan is added.
3. **Level cards are lock screens.** Each level opens on a phone lock screen. A time stamp and one notification slide in: `LEVEL 3 · 4 active plans`. The lock screen gets more cluttered and cracked as the video goes on.
4. **Meme pacing.**
   - Quick reaction punch-ins: SU01's face, a zoom, a label.
   - Freeze-frames with a record scratch: "Yep. That's you."
   - A running group chat with your friend **Jay** (C03), whose texts pop up as commentary.
   - The cart total slams on screen like a fighting-game combo counter: `PLAN ×7!`
5. **Receipt outro per level.** Every level ends with a receipt printing out the running total. The paper gets longer each time, and in the payoff it's a receipt as long as the screen.

**Visuals:** the library plates, poses and cast. One new prop sheet covers the things you buy (see the end). Everything else is drawn in code.

None of that is an accident. Every part of it is designed to make the yes faster than the thinking.

### 4. Level 4: Seven plans, three apps (the stack)

*[Lock screen: LEVEL 4 · 7 active plans. L04 home office. The phone home screen now has THREE generic pay-later app icons, each a different colour.]*

At some point, you hit the limit on the first app. It just won't approve you for more.

So you download a second one. And then a third.

*[P05 tapping. Three icons pulse. TV prop, $480 → App 2. Sofa, $900 → App 3, "12 monthly payments". Tracker: PLANS 7 · APPS 3 · OWED $1,740.]*

This has a name. It's called loan stacking. And it's way more common than you'd think.

When the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau studied buy now, pay later loans, about sixty-three percent of borrowers had more than one loan going at the same time at some point during the year. And a third had borrowed from more than one buy now, pay later company.

*[Stat card: "63% had multiple loans at once · 33% used multiple BNPL lenders". Source pill: CFPB, Jan 2025 (data from 2021–22).]*

And getting approved again is easy. That same CFPB study found that in 2022, these companies approved around seventy-eight percent of loan applications from people with subprime or deep subprime credit. The kind of credit that would get a lot of credit card applications turned down.

*[Stat card: "78% approval rate for subprime / deep subprime applicants (2022)". Source pill: CFPB, Jan 2025. A big green APPROVED stamp hits three times in a row.]*

Which sounds generous. But being approved and being able to afford it are two very different things.

And notice the sofa. That wasn't pay in 4. That was a *monthly* plan, a year long. Some of those longer plans charge interest, sometimes a lot of it. The "zero percent" in the ads doesn't always come along for the ride.

*[Sofa plan card flips over: "APR may apply" in small print, which zooms in comically.]*

Now here's what makes stacking so dangerous. Every app only sees *its own* plans. App one doesn't know about app two. App two doesn't know about app three. And for a long time, most of this didn't show up on your credit report either.

*[Three apps each shown seeing only their own slice of a pie. SU01 sees the whole pie, sweating. P21.]*

Some people call it phantom debt. It's real money you really owe… that nobody, including you, can see all in one place.

*[Jay: "wait u got a NEW tv??" You: "it's only $120 a month bro"]*

*[Receipt: 7 PLANS · 3 APPS · $1,740 LEFT.]*

*[Split screen: a calendar where every week has a food bill, and the old ones stack on top of the new ones like Tetris.]*

And this is not some rare thing. In the Federal Reserve's latest survey of American households, about one in five buy now, pay later users had used it for groceries or food delivery. And twenty-nine percent said they used it because it was the only way they could afford the purchase.

*[Stat card: "1 in 5 BNPL users bought groceries or food delivery" · "29%: 'the only way I could afford it'". Source pill: Federal Reserve SHED, May 2026 (survey Oct 2025).]*

### 6. Level 6: The first late payment

*[Lock screen: LEVEL 6 · 10 active plans · 1 PAST DUE. A red notification badge. L04 home office, morning. P22 yawning with a coffee.]*

It's a Tuesday. Payday is Friday. And at six in the morning, three of your plans try to take their payments… from an account that has eleven dollars in it.

*[On screen: HYPOTHETICAL.]*

The problem is your plans don't know when your payday is. Each one was set up on whatever day you happened to buy the thing. So on some weeks, everything lands *before* your money does.

*[Calendar: payday marked with a gold coin on Friday. Three red payment markers sit on Tuesday, before it. The coin is too late.]*

Two things happen at once. The pay-later app charges you a late fee. And your bank charges you an overdraft fee, because the payment went through anyway and pushed you below zero.

*[Two fee stickers slap onto the screen one after the other: LATE FEE and OVERDRAFT FEE. P11 facepalm.]*

This is exactly where a lot of people end up. In that same Federal Reserve survey, about a quarter of buy now, pay later users had paid late in the past year. Of the people who paid late, sixty-four percent were charged a fee. And eleven percent of all users got hit with an overdraft or insufficient-funds fee because of a buy now, pay later payment.

*[Stat cards, one per beat: "26% of BNPL users paid late" · "64% of late payers were charged a fee" · "11% got overdraft/NSF fees from BNPL". Source pill: Federal Reserve SHED, May 2026.]*

And notice: the "zero percent" was only ever true if *everything* went perfectly. Every payment, every week, for months. Real life doesn't go perfectly.

*[Jay: "yo u still owe me $20 from friday 👀" P21 sweating.]*

*[Lock screen: LEVEL 7. A notification from a credit-monitoring app: "Something changed on your credit report". L08 bank interior. C04 banker at a desk, P01 nervous across from him.]*

For years, the deal with a lot of these apps was: they don't really check your credit, and they don't really report to it.

That's changing.

*[Phone shows a credit report building line by line.]*

In 2025, one of the biggest buy now, pay later companies started reporting *all* of its loans, including pay in 4, to two of the three major credit bureaus. And FICO, the company behind most credit scores, announced new scores built to include buy now, pay later data.

*[Two cards: "Major BNPL lender: all loans, including Pay in 4, now reported to Experian & TransUnion (2025)" · "FICO Score 10 BNPL announced June 2025". Source pills: Affirm press release, Mar 2025; FICO, Jun 2025.]*

It's still patchy. Some companies report, some don't, and lenders don't all use the newest scores yet. But the direction is clear. Your pay-later life is becoming visible.

*[A fog lifts off the "phantom debt" pie from Level 4. All three slices now show.]*

And one thing has always been true: if a debt goes unpaid long enough to get sent to collections, that can show up and drag your credit score down. The kind of score that decides your rent applications, your car loan, sometimes even your phone contract.

*[C04 banker looks at a screen, slowly shakes his head, and slides a form back across the desk. Stamp: DENIED (red). Gavel SFX.]*

And a lower credit score is expensive in ways you don't see straight away. It can mean a higher interest rate on a car loan, a bigger deposit on an apartment, or a no from a landlord before you've even had the chance to explain.

*[Three doors close one after the other: CAR LOAN (higher rate), APARTMENT (bigger deposit), LANDLORD (denied). Thud, thud, thud.]*

Ten plans for sneakers and groceries… and now it's following you into the rest of your life.

### 9. Level 9: Locked out

*[Lock screen: LEVEL 9. The phone screen is shattered. Notifications stacked to the top: this is the cold open. L02 bedroom, 2 AM, P21 on the bed. We've caught up with the start of the video.]*

And that brings us back to two in the morning.

*[The cold open replays in fast-forward: "Payment failed. Payment failed. Late fee."]*

Here's what usually happens next. Miss payments and most apps stop letting you make new purchases. The thing that was always there to catch you… isn't anymore.

*[Big PAY IN 4 button greys out. A padlock drops on it. Clunk.]*

The plans you already have still need paying. The fees are still stacking. And if it goes on for long enough, the debt can be handed to a collections agency, and *that's* the version that's most likely to show up on your credit report.

And honestly, the worst part isn't even the money. It's the feeling. Flinching every time your phone buzzes. Not opening the banking app because you don't want to know. Saying "yeah all good" when it's really not.

### 10. Level 10: Zero plans (the way out)

*[Lock screen: LEVEL 10 · 0 active plans (the goal). Morning light. L03 kitchen, clean. P02 explaining. Calm, warm music.]*

Okay. Deep breath. Here's how people actually climb out of this.

*[P23 presenting with a pointer. Steps pop in one at a time, ticked off with a satisfying click.]*

One: see the whole stack. Open every app and write down every plan: what's left, and when it's due. All of it, in one list. Phantom debt stops being scary the second you can see it.

*[The phone lays all 12 plans out in one list. Total at the bottom: $2,340. HYPOTHETICAL.]*

Two: stop adding. Turn off one-tap checkout. Remove your saved cards from shopping sites. Delete the apps you don't need. Make the next plan *harder* to start than the last one.

*[Three app icons get dragged into the trash one by one. Paper-crumple SFX.]*

Three: protect the essentials first. Rent, utilities, food. Then the plans, starting with whatever is late or charging fees or interest, because that's what's growing.

*[The plans list re-sorts itself, with LATE / FEES / INTEREST jumping to the top.]*

Four: talk to them. A lot of lenders have hardship options, or will move a payment date if you ask *before* you miss it. And if it's gotten really heavy, a nonprofit credit counselor can help you build a plan.

*[P13 on the phone, nodding. Speech bubble: "Can we move my payment date?"]*

*[On screen: "Not financial advice. If you're struggling, look for a nonprofit credit counselor."]*

And there are two classic ways to pay the stack down. The avalanche: put every spare dollar on whatever's costing you the most in interest and fees, and pay the minimum on everything else. Or the snowball: kill the smallest plan first, for the quick win, then roll that money into the next one.

*[Split screen. Left, AVALANCHE: a big snowball of fees rolling downhill and getting smaller. Right, SNOWBALL: little plan cards getting knocked off one by one like bowling pins. Strike SFX.]*

The avalanche usually saves more money. The snowball usually keeps people going. The best one is whichever one you'll actually stick to.

And then, one by one, the plans close.

*[Montage: plan cards turning green and flying off the stack. The Tracker counts down: 12… 9… 5… 2… 0. Each one gets a ding. P10 celebrating at the end.]*

*[Tracker: PLANS 0 · OWED $0.]*

Buy now, pay later isn't evil. Used once, for something you planned to buy anyway, with the money already set aside? It's a free little loan. The problem was never one plan.

It's the stack.

So if you *are* going to use it, here are the rules people who use it safely tend to follow.

*[P20 thumbs up. Three rules land like stickers on the phone screen.]*

Only use it if you could pay the full price *today*, from money you already have.

One plan at a time. Never a stack.

And never for the stuff you eat, the stuff you need to keep the lights on, or the stuff you'll be finished with before the last payment.

*[The third rule glows. The empty fridge appears for half a second, a little foreshadowing.]*

### 11. The payoff

*[Gold gradient. All ten lock screens in a row, from clean to completely shattered. Each lights up as it's named.]*

So: which purchase was the point of no return?

Not the sneakers. One plan you can pay off is fine.

Not the TV or the sofa either. Those hurt, but they were still things you *chose*.

*[The Level 5 lock screen glows red. The empty fridge flashes up.]*

It was the groceries.

The moment buy now, pay later stopped paying for things you *wanted* and started paying for things you *needed*. Because you can always stop buying sneakers. You can't stop eating. From that moment on, every week made the next week worse.

*[The giant receipt unrolls the full height of the screen, then is torn off at Level 5. Rip SFX.]*

If you ever catch yourself splitting groceries, that's not a shortcut anymore. That's the warning light.

*[Jay text: "real talk tho… how many plans u got rn?"]*

So be honest with me: how many buy now, pay later plans do you have open right now? Zero counts. Tell me in the comments. I read all of them.

*[P05 pointing at the viewer.]*

And if you want to see what happens when you're on the *other* side of the money, here's your life at every level of side hustle, from zero to a million.

*[End screen: P03 pointing up-right at the video element (V04).]*
