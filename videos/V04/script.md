## NARRATION

### 0. The hook

*[L01 office cubicle. P13 phone pose, then P09 thinking. Clock prop reads 4:59. Level bar appears empty: LEVEL 0 · $0. HOURS LEFT meter full.]*

You have a job. It pays the bills… mostly.

And like almost everyone with a job, you have an idea. Something you could sell. Something you could do on the side.

By the end of this video, that idea is going to make you a million dollars.

*[Gold gradient. P10 celebrating. Level bar rockets to $1M. Cash-burst particles. Then it snaps back to zero and P10 swaps to P07 shrug.]*

But somewhere between zero and a million, almost everyone quits. Not because they fail. Not because the idea was bad. Because of something way more boring than that.

So here's the question we're answering today: which level kills the most side hustles?

It's not the one you think. And the most *important* level isn't the one you'd guess either.

*[Clock hits 5:00. P16 walking out of frame.]*

Clock's out. Let's go.

### 1. Level 1: $0 (The idea)

*[Chapter card: LEVEL 1 · $0. L02 bedroom at night. P13 phone pose on the bed, phone glow.]*

At zero dollars, a side hustle is mostly… watching videos about side hustles.

*[Phone insert, scrolling: laptops on beaches, stacks of cash, "I made $40K in my sleep". Generic invented gurus only.]*

Everyone online is making money while they sleep. You're not making money while you're awake.

And this is where the first trap lives. At level zero, the easiest person to make money from… is *you*.

*[PR phone close-up: big glowing BUY NOW button. P05 finger hovering, then tap.]*

Courses. Templates. "Secret" strategies. Coaching calls. At level zero, you're not the business. You're the customer.

Then comes the second trap: pretending to work. You spend three weeks designing a logo, picking a name and building a website for a business that has exactly zero customers.

*[P14 at laptop, a giant fancy logo on screen. The level bar stays at $0. Tumbleweed.]*

It *feels* productive. It isn't.

The side hustles that actually get off the ground usually start with something you already have. A skill people pay for at work. A tool sitting in your garage. The thing your friends keep asking you to help with for free.

*[P09 thinking. Thought bubbles pop up one by one: camera, toolbox, laptop with code, cake.]*

Not the hottest hustle on the internet. The one you could start *this weekend*.

And you can test it in a weekend too. Before you build anything, tell ten people what you're offering and what it costs. Post it. Message people. Put up a simple page.

*[P13 phone pose. Ten little speech bubbles fly out of the phone. Most come back with "…", one comes back with a coin.]*

If nobody bites, you've lost a weekend, not a year. If somebody does, you've got your first customer before you've even finished the logo.

So here's the only move that matters at Level 1: stop researching, and sell one thing. Anything.

### 2. Level 2: $1 (The first sale)

*[Level-up whoosh + gold flash. Chapter card: LEVEL 2 · $1. L03 kitchen. P19 holding a parcel.]*

Then one night… it happens.

*[PR phone buzzes on the table: notification bubble with a coin icon. P08 shocked.]*

A complete stranger, someone who owes you absolutely nothing, gives you money for something you made.

*[P10 celebrating, on top of a kitchen chair. Confetti.]*

After the platform takes its cut, it's maybe a dollar of actual profit. It feels like a million.

And you're in good company. In Bankrate's 2025 survey, over a quarter of people with a side hustle made between one and fifty dollars a month.

*[On screen: "28% of side hustlers earn $1–$50 a month" (Bankrate, June 2025).]*

So yes, it's small. But that feeling you have right now is the most important thing in this entire video.

Because it proves the one thing no course, no logo and no business plan can prove: a real person will pay you.

*[P03 pointing up at the level bar, which reads $1 in glowing gold.]*

Most ideas never get here. Yours just did. Remember this level. We're coming back to it.

### 3. Level 3: $200 a month (The median)

*[Level up. Chapter card: LEVEL 3 · $200/MONTH. L03 kitchen, now covered in stock and sticky notes (NEW scene plate). P14 at laptop.]*

A few months in, you're making around two hundred dollars a month.

That might not sound like much, but it's actually right in the middle of the pack.

About one in four American adults had a side hustle in Bankrate's 2025 survey. For Gen Z it was more like one in three. And the *median* side hustler made two hundred dollars a month.

*[On screen: "27% of US adults have a side hustle · Gen Z: 34% · Median: $200/month" (Bankrate, June 2025).]*

But the *average* was eight hundred and eighty-five dollars.

*[On screen: two bars. Median $200 vs Average $885.]*

When the average is more than four times the median, a small group of people is making a *lot*, and dragging the average up. Most people are making… lunch money.

*[P07 shrug with a sandwich prop.]*

And lunch money matters. Thirty-five percent of side hustlers in that survey said they use it to cover regular living expenses. Not holidays. Rent. Groceries. Bills.

*[On screen: "35% use it for regular expenses" (Bankrate, June 2025).]*

Now here's the math nobody posts on social media. Take what you made and divide it by the hours you actually put in.

*[P12 counting on fingers. On screen: HYPOTHETICAL. $200 ÷ 20 hours = $10/hour.]*

Made-up example: two hundred dollars for twenty hours of work is ten dollars an hour.

*[P11 facepalm.]*

Congratulations. You've invented a part-time job with no boss… but also no guaranteed pay, no sick days and nobody to complain to.

And that's because of *what kind* of side hustle you picked. There are really only three.

*[P23 presenting. Three cards flip in one at a time: a clock, a box, a gear.]*

One: you sell your *time*. Tutoring, freelancing, driving, fixing things. It pays from day one, but you can only ever earn as many hours as you have.

Two: you sell *stuff*. You buy or make things and sell them for more. More room to grow, but now you've got stock, shipping and returns.

Three: you sell something you make *once*. A template, a course, a video, an app. It can sell a thousand times without a thousand times the work. But it's the slowest to get going, and most never take off.

*[The clock card shakes. The gear card glows faintly.]*

Most people start with type one, because it's the fastest way to your first dollar. Remember that clock. It's about to become a problem.

### 4. Level 4: $400 (The taxman notices)

*[Level up. Chapter card: LEVEL 4 · $400. L03 kitchen. C01 taxman walks in and taps P01 on the shoulder. Swap to P08 shocked.]*

Congratulations. You've attracted your first follower.

In the US, once your net earnings from self-employment hit four hundred dollars in a year, you generally have to file and pay self-employment tax. That's fifteen point three percent, for Social Security and Medicare, on top of regular income tax.

*[On screen: "Self-employment tax: 15.3% once net earnings reach $400 a year" (IRS).]*

At your day job, you never really noticed this, because your employer quietly paid half of it for you. Now *you're* the employer. So you pay both halves.

*[P18 holding coins, splitting them into two stacks. Hands both to C01. C01 nods politely.]*

There's good news, though. The key word is *net*. That's what's left after legitimate business expenses. Supplies, software, shipping, the share of your phone bill you actually use for the business.

*[PR shoebox overflowing with receipts. P10 hugging it like a teddy bear.]*

So from this level on, you keep every receipt. And a lot of people start moving a slice of every sale into a separate pot for tax. That way April isn't a jump scare.

*[On screen: HYPOTHETICAL. Every $100 sale → $25 into a "TAX" jar.]*

Your hobby has a tax bill now. That's how you know it's real.

### 5. Level 5: $10,000 a year (The time wall)

*[Level up. Chapter card: LEVEL 5 · $10,000/YEAR. Split screen: L01 office by day (P14) and L02 bedroom by night (P14). HOURS LEFT meter drains fast and flashes red.]*

Now you're making around ten thousand dollars a year on the side. That's real money. For a lot of people, that's a holiday, a car repair fund and an emergency fund, all at once.

It's also a second job.

*[PR calendar: every evening and weekend square fills in. PR phone: a friend texts "you coming tonight?" P13 types "can't".]*

Evenings: gone. Weekends: gone. Friends: "Are you still alive?"

*[HOURS LEFT meter hits almost empty. Alarm SFX.]*

Think about the hours. A week has one hundred and sixty-eight of them. Take off sleep. Take off your day job. Take off commuting, eating, showering and existing. What's left is maybe a few hours a day. Now your hustle wants all of them.

*[On screen: 168 hours. Chunks removed one by one: SLEEP, JOB, COMMUTE, LIFE. A tiny sliver left: HUSTLE.]*

And here's the cruel part. At your day job, when you work harder, you get tired. At your side hustle, when you work harder, you get tired *and* you get more orders. Success makes the problem *worse*.

*[P22 yawning, then P15 slumped on the floor. Tiny "zzz".]*

This is the time wall. It's where a huge number of side hustles quietly die. The idea wasn't bad. The customers were real. The hustler just… ran out of hours.

The people who get past this level usually do three things.

*[P23 presenting with a pointer. Three icons pop in one at a time.]*

One: they raise their prices. That feels terrifying. But if you're busy, you're probably too cheap. Every hour has to be worth more.

Two: they drop the customers who take the most time for the least money. Every hustle has a few. You know exactly who they are.

Three: they automate or outsource the boring stuff. Templates, scheduling tools, a freelancer for the jobs they hate.

*[P16 walking, shoving a pile of boring tasks into a box marked OUTSOURCE. HOURS LEFT meter refills a bit. Satisfying SFX.]*

At Level 5, the whole game changes. You stop selling your *time*… and start building a *system*.

### 6. Level 6: $20,000 (The paperwork level)

*[Level up. Chapter card: LEVEL 6 · $20,000. L04 home office. PR envelope slides in marked 1099-K. P21 sweating, opening it like it might explode.]*

Once you pass twenty thousand dollars in payments *and* two hundred transactions through a payment platform in a year, that platform will usually send you, and the IRS, a form called a 1099-K.

*[On screen: "1099-K: over $20,000 AND 200+ transactions (from 2025)" (IRS / One Big Beautiful Bill Act).]*

To be clear, the IRS already expected you to report *all* of your income, form or no form. Now there's just a paper trail.

This is the level where the hobby grows up.

*[P02 explaining. Checklist ticks in one by one.]*

You open a separate business bank account, so business money and pizza money stop mixing. You start paying estimated taxes during the year, instead of getting one horrible surprise in April. Plenty of people set up an LLC around here too, to separate the business from their personal life.

Quick reality check on the LLC, though. It's a legal wrapper, not a magic tax trick. It doesn't make your taxes disappear, and it doesn't make you look like a big company. What it mostly does is draw a line between "the business" and "you", which starts to matter a lot once real money and real customers are involved.

*[PR envelope stamped with a simple house on one side and a shop on the other, a line drawn between them. P02 explaining.]*

*[P06 arms crossed, now wearing a tiny tie (NEW pose variant or tie prop overlay). Mirror frame.]*

You're not "doing a little thing on the side" anymore. You're running a business. And it's time you started acting like it.

### Quick recap

*[Gold gradient. The six level icons so far light up in a row as the narrator names them. P03 pointing along them.]*

Quick catch-up.

Level 1: you stopped buying courses and picked an idea.
Level 2: a stranger paid you.
Level 3: you realized you were earning about ten bucks an hour.
Level 4: the taxman showed up.
Level 5: you hit the time wall, and built a system.
Level 6: the paperwork arrived.

Now it gets interesting. Because from here, the problems aren't about money anymore. They're about *people*.

### 7. Level 7: $3,000 a month (Lifestyle creep)

*[Level up. Chapter card: LEVEL 7 · $3,000/MONTH. L05 street. P24 relaxed on a bench, then a shiny new car pulls up (PR car).]*

Three thousand dollars a month. On the side. That's thirty-six thousand a year.

So naturally, you reward yourself.

*[PR car, PR phone, PR takeaway bags pile up around P06.]*

Newer car. Better phone. Nicer apartment. More takeaway, because who has time to cook when you're running an empire?

*[On screen: HYPOTHETICAL. Hustle income $3,000/month · new monthly costs $2,800.]*

And one day you realize something scary. Your side hustle isn't *extra* money anymore. It's holding up your life.

*[P21 sweating. A tower of monthly bills stacked on top of a tiny pillar marked HUSTLE. It wobbles.]*

This is lifestyle creep. It's sneaky, because every single upgrade felt reasonable. But now you *can't* have a slow month. You *can't* take a break. The hustle owns you.

The people who avoid this usually treat hustle money as a different kind of money. It goes to building the business, to savings, to paying off debt. Anything except quietly raising the cost of your normal life.

*[P02 explaining. The HUSTLE pillar gets thicker as money flows into a SAVINGS jar instead.]*

Because in a couple of levels, you're going to want the freedom to make a big decision. And lifestyle creep is how people lose that freedom without noticing.

### 8. Level 8: $50,000 (The copycats)

*[Level up. Chapter card: LEVEL 8 · $50,000. L06 market (NEW location). P01 behind a stall. Three C05 copycat vendors pop up around him with identical stalls.]*

Fifty thousand a year. People have noticed you.

Unfortunately, some of those people are competitors.

The moment something makes money in public, someone copies it. Same product. Same photos. Sometimes the exact same words in the description. And a lower price.

*[Copycats' price tags drop: $20 → $15 → $9. SU01's sales line wobbles.]*

This is where a lot of people panic and start a price war. Prices go down, and down, and down. That's a race to the bottom, and the winner is whoever can survive earning the least.

*[P11 facepalm.]*

The hustles that make it through do something else. They build the one thing a copycat can't copy: a reason to pick *you*.

*[Queue of C02 customers at SU01's stall holding up speech bubbles with stars and hearts. P20 thumbs up.]*

Reviews. Repeat customers. Faster replies. A little handwritten thank-you in the box. People who know your name and tell their friends about you.

That's called a brand. Right now, it's the most valuable thing you own. And it doesn't show up anywhere on the level bar.

Which is good timing. Because the next level is the one that changes your whole life, and you're going to need every bit of that brand behind you.

### 9. Level 9: $100,000 (The quit-your-job level)

*[Level up, big gold flash. Chapter card: LEVEL 9 · $100,000. L01 office. P01 at the desk. PR resignation letter on the desk.]*

A hundred thousand dollars a year. Your side hustle now makes as much as your job. Maybe more.

And you face the biggest decision in this entire video.

Do you quit?

*[Fork in the road (NEW plate). Two blank signposts, labeled in the edit: SAFE and FREE. P09 thinking in the middle.]*

It's so tempting. No boss. No commute. No meetings that should've been emails.

But your job gives you a lot of things that don't show up on your paycheck.

*[Items float away from the SAFE sign one at a time: a steady pay slip, a calendar with a beach (paid time off), a first-aid kit (health insurance in the US), a piggy bank (retirement match).]*

A steady income, even in a bad month. Paid time off. In the US, usually health insurance. Often a retirement plan with an employer match.

Quit, and you have to replace *all* of that yourself, out of the hustle.

*[On screen: HYPOTHETICAL.]*

A common approach is to build a runway first. Made-up example: save enough to cover six to twelve months of living costs. Watch the hustle keep hitting its numbers for a while. *Then* jump.

Because your hustle might have a bad month. Your rent won't.

*[P02 explaining with a calendar prop showing 6–12 blank months.]*

This is exactly why Level 7 mattered. If lifestyle creep ate your hustle money, you can't build a runway. If you saved it, you can.

And it doesn't have to be all or nothing. Some people ask to go part-time first, or to switch to four days a week. Some take unpaid leave to test life without the paycheck. Some keep the job and hire help for the hustle instead.

*[The fork in the road grows a third, smaller path in the middle, labeled in the edit: PART-TIME.]*

There's no right answer. There's just the one that matches your runway, and your nerve.

*[P05 hands the resignation letter to C07 boss. The office (C08 coworkers) slow-claps. SFX.]*

You jump.

### 10. Level 10: $500,000 (You're the boss now)

*[Level up. Chapter card: LEVEL 10 · $500,000. L07 small studio office. Three cast members working. P24 at the head of a table.]*

Half a million dollars a year. You've hired people.

And here's the plot twist nobody warns you about: the thing you loved doing? You barely do it anymore.

*[PR calendar: MEETING. PAYROLL. MEETING. HIRING. MEETING. P21 sweating, surrounded by paper.]*

Your job now is payroll, hiring, training, managing, and fixing whatever broke today. You started a business so you'd never have a boss. Now you *are* one, and it's harder than it looked.

So who do you hire first? A lot of owners say the same thing: hire for the job you're *worst* at, or the one you hate most. If you're great at making the product but awful at emails, your first hire answers the emails.

*[P09 thinking. Three job cards: MAKING, EMAILS, ACCOUNTS. The EMAILS card gets stamped HIRED.]*

Because every hour you spend on the thing you're bad at is an hour you're *not* spending on the thing only you can do.

And you learn the most important lesson in business: revenue isn't profit.

*[P23 presenting. Waterfall chart: HYPOTHETICAL. Revenue $500,000 → staff, stock, software, rent, taxes −$350,000 → Profit $150,000.]*

Made-up example: five hundred thousand dollars comes in. Staff, stock, software, rent and taxes take three hundred and fifty thousand of it. What's actually *yours* is a hundred and fifty.

That's still a great result. But it's not what the level bar says.

*[The level bar reading $500,000 shrinks down to show $150,000 underneath. P07 shrug.]*

Whenever someone online says "my business made half a million this year", this is the question to ask: made… or *kept*?

### 11. Level 11: $1,000,000 (The real million)

*[Level up. The biggest flash yet. Chapter card: LEVEL 11 · $1,000,000. L07 studio, now sleek. P24 relaxed at a desk.]*

A million dollars.

Here's the thing most "I built a million-dollar side hustle" stories leave out. That million is usually *revenue*. A million coming in. Not a million in your pocket.

So how does a side hustle actually make someone a millionaire?

Often… by selling it.

*[C04 buyer in suit (V01 banker) walks in with PR briefcase. P08 shocked, eyebrows above sunglasses.]*

Buyers don't pay for one year of profit. They pay for a business that will *keep* making profit, ideally without you in it. That's why the systems you built at Level 5 and the brand you built at Level 8 suddenly matter so much. They're what make the business *sellable*.

*[On screen: HYPOTHETICAL. Yearly profit $300,000 × 3 = $900,000 sale price.]*

Made-up example: if a buyer paid three times a yearly profit of three hundred thousand dollars, that's nine hundred thousand dollars for something that started on your kitchen table. Real prices vary a *lot* depending on the business.

And before they pay a cent, a buyer goes through everything.

*[C04 buyer flicking through a thick folder with a magnifying glass. P21 sweating.]*

Are the profits real, and can you prove them? Remember those receipts from Level 4? Those separate accounts from Level 6? This is where they pay off. Do the customers come back? Would the whole thing collapse if *you* disappeared for a month?

If the answer to that last one is yes, you don't own a business. You own a job with extra paperwork. And nobody pays much for that.

*[PR briefcase opens. Gold glow on P10 celebrating.]*

Remember our very first video? The richest people in the world don't just own yachts. They own boring businesses that make money whether they show up or not.

*[V01 warehouse frame flashes up for a second.]*

Your side hustle just became one of those.

### 12. The payoff

*[Gold gradient. All eleven level icons in a row: phone, parcel, laptop, taxman, calendar, envelope, car, copycat stalls, resignation letter, team, briefcase.]*

So, which level kills the most side hustles?

Not Level 1. At zero, you haven't really started.

Not the million. By then, you've basically won.

*[The calendar icon at Level 5 glows red. HOURS LEFT meter drains to zero.]*

It's the time wall. Somewhere around a few hundred to a few thousand dollars a month, when it stops being exciting and starts eating every evening you've got.

That's where most people stop. Not because the idea was bad, but because they never turned their *time* into a *system*.

*[The car icon at Level 7 glows orange.]*

And the sneakiest killer is lifestyle creep, because it doesn't feel like failing. It feels like winning.

*[The Level 2 parcel icon glows gold. P03 pointing at it.]*

But the most *important* level? It's the one dollar.

Because once one stranger pays you, you know it's possible. Everything after that is just doing it again… bigger… and smarter.

So what's *your* side hustle, and what level are you on right now? Tell me in the comments. I read all of them.

*[P05 pointing at the viewer.]*

And if you want to see what happens when the money *really* stacks up, here's every level of wealth, all the way to a hundred billion dollars.

*[End screen: P03 pointing up-right at the video element.]*

## RESEARCH AND EDITORIAL NOTES (NOT SPOKEN)

**Editorial angle:** Level-by-level POV. The hook asks which level kills the most side hustles, and the payoff answers it: the time wall (Level 5), with lifestyle creep (Level 7) as the sneakiest killer and the $1 first sale as the most important level. Two open loops from the hook ("which kills most", "most important") keep people watching to the end.
